# Benchmark-Driven Short-Horizon Forecasting over Open Gridded Air-Quality Fields for Albanian Cities

## Abstract

Urban air pollution remains a persistent challenge for environmental management, especially in cities with limited monitoring infrastructure and limited access to proprietary forecasting services. This study develops and evaluates a benchmark-driven workflow for short-term air-quality forecasting and alerting in eight Albanian cities using openly available gridded air-quality and meteorological data. Hourly inputs were harmonized into a daily dataset containing 163,008 hourly records and 6,792 city-day observations from 1 January 2024 to 28 April 2026. Forecasts were generated for daily mean PM2.5 and daily maximum European Air Quality Index (AQI) at 1-, 2-, and 3-day horizons. Local univariate baselines were compared with pooled cross-city feature-based models under a common rolling-origin backtesting design. For daily maximum AQI, the pooled benchmark reduced mean absolute error from 3.12 to 2.82 at 1 day, from 4.15 to 3.84 at 2 days, and from 4.49 to 4.29 at 3 days. PM2.5 results were mixed, with short-horizon gains but stronger ARIMA performance at 2 and 3 days. The workflow also produced illustrative city alert rankings and forecast snapshots for operational interpretation.

**Index Terms:** air quality forecasting, PM2.5, AQI, machine learning, early warning, Albania, reproducible benchmark.

## I. Introduction

Air pollution remains a major threat to public health and urban sustainability. Recent European evidence shows that exposure to multiple air pollutants frequently occurs in combination rather than isolation, making compound episodes a persistent feature of urban risk (Chen et al., 2024). The health burden remains substantial at the pollutant-specific level as well: recent work has quantified a large mortality burden associated with ozone across Europe (Achebak et al., 2024), while epidemiological analysis continues to show that harmful cardiovascular effects can be detected even below current regulatory limits (Wang et al., 2024). In Albania, recent journal evidence on long-term biomonitoring likewise suggests that air-quality assessment remains an active and relevant environmental challenge, reinforcing the need for stronger local analytical tools (Lazo et al., 2024).

In this context, short-term air-quality forecasting is not merely a prediction exercise; it is part of operational risk management. Public authorities and municipal analysts need forecasts that are timely enough to support warning communication, transparent enough to justify interventions, and reproducible enough to be trusted over repeated updates. These requirements are especially important in settings where dense monitoring infrastructure, proprietary forecasting services, or long station histories are limited. For such settings, the central question is not only whether machine learning can improve forecast accuracy, but whether an end-to-end open-data workflow can support credible and interpretable city-level alerting over gridded environmental data.

Recent research has expanded rapidly in both algorithmic and operational directions. Review studies show a field that has moved from classical statistical baselines toward machine learning, deep learning, and spatiotemporal hybrid systems, while also highlighting recurring weaknesses in validation design, comparability across case studies, interpretability, and reproducibility (Mendez et al., 2023; Agbehadji & Obagbuwa, 2024; Houdou et al., 2024). Parallel work on automated forecasting systems, web-facing predictive services, and forecasting methodology further emphasizes that practical usefulness depends not only on model accuracy, but also on leakage-aware evaluation, strong baselines, stable multi-step performance, and workflows that can be rerun transparently as new data arrive (Ke et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2024; Hewamalage et al., 2023; Januschowski et al., 2022; Makridakis et al., 2022; Strom & Gundersen, 2024; "Moving towards reproducible machine learning," 2021). Yet there remains limited evidence on how these methodological principles can be integrated into a practical early-warning workflow for a relatively small set of cities using openly accessible gridded environmental data.

This article addresses that gap by developing a benchmark-driven forecasting workflow for Albania using open gridded environmental data. The study makes four contributions. First, it constructs a reproducible city-level analytical pipeline from open gridded air-quality and weather data for eight Albanian cities. Second, it benchmarks short-horizon forecasting for daily mean PM2.5 and daily maximum European AQI using both local univariate baselines and pooled feature-based models. Third, it assesses whether cross-city learning with structured temporal and meteorological features yields useful benchmark gains relative to simpler baselines. Fourth, it converts benchmark outcomes into an illustrative alerting layer through champion selection, alert scoring, and city attention ranking. Rather than assuming that a single model should be deployed everywhere, the study asks a more operationally useful question: which model family should be trusted for a given target and forecast horizon in a data-constrained urban workflow?

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. Section II reviews the most relevant literature and positions the study within current machine-learning and operational air-quality research. The following sections describe the data pipeline, forecasting design, benchmark results, and operational early-warning outputs.

## II. Related Work

Recent review papers provide a clear overview of the field's direction. Air-quality forecasting research has moved from classical statistical baselines toward machine learning, deep learning, and spatiotemporal hybrid systems (Mendez et al., 2023; Agbehadji & Obagbuwa, 2024). At the same time, systematic reviews point to recurring weaknesses in the literature, including inconsistent validation protocols, limited comparability across case studies, and insufficient integration of interpretability into predictive workflows (Mendez et al., 2023; Agbehadji & Obagbuwa, 2024; Houdou et al., 2024). These observations are directly relevant to our study because they suggest that methodological discipline and reproducibility remain as important as algorithmic novelty.

Representative application studies also show that forecasting gains are highly context-dependent. Petric et al. (2024) compared ensemble machine-learning, deep-learning, and time-series approaches for hourly pollutant forecasting and found that performance varied strongly by pollutant and model family rather than following a simple complexity hierarchy. Ozupak et al. (2025) likewise reported that comparative and ensemble strategies can improve results, but not in a way that makes a single method universally dominant. Rajesh et al. (2025) proposed a machine-learning framework that combines forecasting with environmental-health risk mapping, while Makhdoomi et al. (2025) used machine learning to support virtual monitoring-station logic for PM2.5 prediction. Liu et al. (2024) focused on AQI class prediction using machine-learning methods over monitoring-based data and secondary modeling. Taken together, these studies support the view that air-quality prediction is increasingly embedded in broader decision-support or monitoring workflows rather than treated only as a standalone regression task.

Recent studies on PM2.5 and AQI forecasting have pushed the methodological frontier further. Gao et al. (2024) combined deep learning, optimization, and ensemble forecasting for PM2.5 prediction. Hu et al. (2024) proposed a federated deep-learning framework for multi-urban PM2.5 forecasting, while Cheng et al. (2025) developed a Transformer-BiLSTM pipeline for AQI forecasting. Other work has explored optimization-enhanced machine-learning prediction (Cengil, 2025), ultrahigh spatiotemporal PM2.5 modeling supported by low-cost sensors and machine learning (Li et al., 2025), particle-swarm-optimized neural models for daily PM2.5 (Liu et al., 2025), attention-based convolutional BiLSTM architectures (Meng et al., 2025), decomposition-driven deep-learning approaches for urban PM2.5 forecasting (Zaini et al., 2022), and hybrid deep models for regional air-quality prediction (Hu et al., 2025). These studies demonstrate strong innovation in predictive architecture, but they also highlight a persistent gap between model sophistication and deployment simplicity in data-constrained environments.

Another important strand concerns operationalization. Ke et al. (2022) developed an automated forecasting system based on machine learning, illustrating how model search and deployment logic can be integrated into routine air-quality forecasting. Rahman et al. (2024) proposed a predictive machine-learning model exposed through a web interface, showing the continued shift from isolated experiments toward service-oriented forecasting systems. For our purposes, the key lesson is that a useful early-warning framework must connect model evaluation to downstream action rather than stop at point prediction alone.

The forecasting-methodology literature provides additional guidance for such system design. Hewamalage et al. (2023) detail common pitfalls in time-series evaluation, including leakage, weak baselines, and misleading comparisons when temporal structure is not handled carefully. Januschowski et al. (2022) show that tree-based global forecasting models can be highly competitive when time-series information is converted into disciplined tabular features. Lessons from the M5 competition reinforce the idea that global machine-learning methods can outperform classical models in many settings without eliminating the value of strong baselines (Makridakis et al., 2022). Recent work on multi-step performance metrics also shows that horizon-wise evaluation can reveal stability and ranking patterns that aggregate accuracy alone may hide (Strom & Gundersen, 2024). Beyond forecasting metrics, reproducible machine-learning workflows have become a broader methodological expectation in modern data science ("Moving towards reproducible machine learning," 2021).

Taken together, these strands point to a gap between increasingly sophisticated predictive architectures and reproducible, operationally interpretable workflows for smaller open-data settings. The unresolved issue is not only which model performs best in principle, but how local baselines, pooled feature-based models, and downstream alerting logic can be connected through explicit benchmark evidence over gridded environmental data. This is the space in which the present study is situated.

## III. Data and Processing Pipeline

### A. Data Sources and Geographic Scope

The study covers eight Albanian cities: Tirane, Durres, Elbasan, Shkoder, Vlore, Fier, Korce, and Berat. Hourly air-quality data were retrieved from the Open-Meteo Air Quality API (Open-Meteo, 2026a), while hourly meteorological covariates were obtained from the Open-Meteo Historical Weather API (Open-Meteo, 2026b). In the implementation used for this study, air-quality requests were issued to the `https://air-quality-api.open-meteo.com/v1/air-quality` endpoint with the `cams_europe` domain, and historical weather requests were issued to the `https://archive-api.open-meteo.com/v1/archive` endpoint. All requests used city coordinates in the WGS84 system and the `Europe/Tirane` timezone.

The air-quality dataset included hourly PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide, ozone, sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide, the overall European AQI, and pollutant-specific AQI sub-indices for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, O3, and SO2 (Open-Meteo, 2026a). The meteorological dataset included hourly near-surface air temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, wind speed, wind direction, and mean sea-level pressure (Open-Meteo, 2026b). The analysis covered the period from 1 January 2024 to 28 April 2026.

The Open-Meteo documentation states that its European air-quality service is based on CAMS European air-quality products at approximately 0.1 degrees spatial resolution, while the historical weather service is based on reanalysis-driven meteorological products (Open-Meteo, 2026a, 2026b). Accordingly, the present framework should be interpreted as an open gridded-data workflow rather than a replacement for regulatory station measurements. No regulatory station measurements were used for model fitting or external evaluation in this study. The AQI interpretation follows the European AQI categorization used by Open-Meteo and is aligned with the broader EEA AQI communication framework (European Environment Agency, 2026; Open-Meteo, 2026a).

### B. Hourly Harmonization

Hourly air-quality and meteorological data were downloaded separately for each city and merged into a single hourly analytical table using city and timestamp as matching keys. The merged dataset was standardized programmatically: timestamps were parsed into datetime format, calendar variables (year, month, day, and hour) were derived, and pollutant, AQI, and meteorological fields were converted to numeric format, with invalid entries treated as missing values. Wind direction was retained in the raw weather extract but was not used in the final daily forecasting layer.

### C. Daily Aggregation and Derived Indicators

Because the forecasting task focused on short-horizon operational warning, the hourly data were aggregated to daily resolution. Daily means and maxima were computed for PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide, ozone, sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide, and AQI-related variables. For meteorological variables, the pipeline retained daily mean, minimum, and maximum temperature, mean relative humidity, daily precipitation total, mean and maximum wind speed, mean sea-level pressure, and the number of hourly observations contributing to each city-day. In the retained dataset, all 6,792 city-day observations were based on 24 hourly records, so no additional daily completeness filtering was required. A concise summary of the aggregated dataset is provided in Supplementary Table S3, the full variable dictionary is provided in Supplementary Table S4, and the complete daily analytical dataset used in the forecasting experiments is provided as Supplementary Data S1.

Daily AQI labels were assigned from the daily maximum European AQI using the categories Good, Fair, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor, and Extremely Poor (European Environment Agency, 2026; Open-Meteo, 2026a). A numeric AQI severity level was used for ranking and event detection. We defined a pollution episode as a day with AQI level at least Poor and a high-risk day as a day with AQI level at least Very Poor. Dominant pollutant pressure was identified from the daily mean pollutant-specific AQI components. In addition, the pipeline generated threshold-based screening flags for selected pollutants. The resulting daily dataset formed the basis for forecasting, city summaries, and operational alert generation.

## IV. Forecasting Methodology

### A. Targets and Evaluation Design

The forecasting layer predicts two daily targets: daily mean PM2.5 and daily maximum European AQI. These targets were chosen because PM2.5 captures an important pollutant-specific burden, whereas daily maximum AQI directly supports public-facing risk communication. Forecasts were generated for 1-, 2-, and 3-day horizons.

In both benchmark families, forecasts were issued after the daily summary for the issue date had become available. Accordingly, the 1- to 3-day forecasts should be interpreted as next-day and short-horizon forecasts conditional on the completed issue-day daily aggregates, rather than as intraday nowcasts.

Evaluation followed a rolling-origin backtesting design consistent with recent guidance on time-series benchmarking and leakage-aware forecast assessment (Hewamalage et al., 2023; Januschowski et al., 2022; Makridakis et al., 2022; Strom & Gundersen, 2024). Both benchmark families used a 365-day historical lookback and forecasting origins that advanced in 28-day steps. At every origin, only information available up to the issue date was used to produce 1-, 2-, and 3-day-ahead predictions. Under the available study period, each city-model-horizon summary was based on 18 rolling-origin forecast instances. Performance was evaluated at the city level using MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and sMAPE. For cross-city benchmark summaries, models were compared primarily by mean MAE, while mean MAE rank was retained as a secondary descriptive diagnostic.

### B. Baseline Local Models

The first benchmark compares five local univariate forecasting methods, each fitted separately for every city-target series:

1. Naive persistence, which repeats the most recent observed value at all forecast horizons  
2. Drift, which extrapolates a linear change from the first and last values in the training window  
3. Holt damped trend, implemented as additive exponential smoothing with a damped trend component  
4. ARIMA with restricted order search  
5. Random Forest with seven lagged target observations

For ARIMA, the candidate orders were (1,1,0), (1,1,1), and (0,1,1), and the converged specification with the lowest AIC was selected. If all candidate ARIMA fits failed, the pipeline fell back to the naive forecast. The Random Forest model used seven lagged target values and a sequential time index as predictors, with 120 trees, a minimum leaf size of 2, and `random_state = 42`; multi-step forecasts were generated recursively. This baseline family intentionally combines transparent reference methods, established statistical time-series models, and a simple non-linear machine-learning comparator, reflecting best-practice recommendations that stronger models should be judged against credible yet interpretable alternatives (Hewamalage et al., 2023; Januschowski et al., 2022; Makridakis et al., 2022).

In practical terms, the baseline benchmark followed a one-city, one-model design: each model was trained separately on the historical series of a single city and did not borrow information from the other cities.

### C. Enhanced Global Benchmark

The second benchmark strengthens the modeling layer through pooled learning across cities. Instead of fitting each city independently, the enhanced benchmark reformulates forecasting as a horizon-specific supervised learning problem with structured features, in line with global tabular forecasting ideas discussed in recent methodology work (Januschowski et al., 2022; Makridakis et al., 2022). For each target and horizon, three models are evaluated:

1. Persistence Current, which uses the current target value as the forecast  
2. Ridge Global Features  
3. HistGradientBoosting Global Features

Feature construction begins with the current target value and autoregressive lags at 1, 2, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days. The pipeline then adds rolling means, rolling standard deviations, and rolling maxima over 3-, 7-, 14-, and 28-day windows computed from lagged history only. Calendar features include day of week, month, day of year, a weekend indicator, and sinusoidal seasonal encodings. Meteorological covariates include daily mean and maximum temperature, mean relative humidity, daily precipitation total, mean and maximum wind speed, and mean sea-level pressure. Finally, cross-target context is added through related pollutant variables: the PM2.5 models receive AQI, PM10, NO2, and O3 context, whereas the AQI models receive PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and O3 context. City indicators are one-hot encoded so that pooled learning can capture location-specific differences while sharing information across series.

The enhanced benchmark uses direct forecasting, meaning that a separate supervised target is defined for each horizon by shifting the city series forward by 1, 2, or 3 days. Evaluation again preserves chronology: for each horizon, training rows are restricted to observations whose target dates fall within the previous 365 days, the origin advances in 28-day steps, and a minimum of 400 pooled training rows is required before model fitting. The Ridge model is estimated in a standardized pipeline with `alpha = 2.0`, whereas HistGradientBoosting is trained with `learning_rate = 0.05`, `max_depth = 4`, `max_iter = 120`, `min_samples_leaf = 20`, and `random_state = 42`.

Thus, unlike the local baseline benchmark, the enhanced benchmark used one pooled model per target and forecast horizon, trained jointly on rows from all cities within the rolling training window while retaining city identity through one-hot indicators.

### D. Supplementary XGBoost Comparative Experiment

To examine whether a different tree-based global learner could challenge the existing enhanced benchmark without changing the feature design, a supplementary XGBoost Global Features experiment was conducted. The model used the same pooled feature set, the same 365-day rolling training window, the same 28-day origin stride, and the same 1-, 2-, and 3-day forecast horizons as the main enhanced benchmark. The fixed XGBoost configuration used `n_estimators = 300`, `learning_rate = 0.05`, `max_depth = 4`, `min_child_weight = 5.0`, `subsample = 0.9`, `colsample_bytree = 0.9`, and `tree_method = hist`.

To avoid optimistic tuning, a separate time-split XGBoost experiment was also conducted. For this analysis, the 18 rolling forecast origins were divided chronologically into 12 development origins (30 December 2024 to 3 November 2025) and 6 later evaluation origins (1 December 2025 to 20 April 2026). XGBoost hyperparameters were selected only on the development origins, and the chosen configuration was then evaluated only on the held-out origins against both the default XGBoost specification and the development-selected enhanced incumbent model.

### E. Champion Selection and Operational Alerts

The operational layer does not assume that one model family is always superior. Instead, for each city, target, and forecast horizon, it compares the best local baseline model with the best enhanced model using city-level backtest MAE and selects the lower-error family as the operational champion. This champion-selection rule transforms benchmark results into a deployment decision that is explicitly target-dependent and horizon-dependent.

The selected forecasts are then mapped into an alert framework. The primary signal is predicted daily maximum AQI, while predicted daily mean PM2.5 acts as a supporting signal relative to the historical 90th and 95th percentiles of PM2.5 for each city. For the fixed case-study snapshot reported in this article, those PM2.5 reference percentiles were computed from each city's full daily PM2.5 history available up to the forecast issue date of 28 April 2026. AQI values are converted to categorical labels and severity scores, and PM2.5 can escalate the alert when it reaches elevated or extreme levels relative to local historical conditions. The final alert scale comprises Stable, Watch, Warning, High, Severe, and Critical. Table 1 summarizes the study-defined operational mapping used in this article. AQI provides the default alert ladder, whereas city-relative PM2.5 acts only as an escalation rule: values at or above the city-specific 90th percentile can promote the alert to High, values at or above the 95th percentile can promote it to Severe, and the joint case of Very Poor AQI with extreme PM2.5 is escalated to Critical. For each city, the strongest alert within the 1- to 3-day forecast window is used to create city attention rankings, heatmap tables, and recommended actions. In this way, the workflow links benchmarked forecasting performance to a transparent alerting prototype rather than treating prediction as an end in itself.

## V. Results

### A. Baseline Benchmark Results

The baseline local benchmark established a strong reference point for both forecasting targets. Table 2 summarizes the best-performing baseline model for each target and forecast horizon after aggregating city-level backtest results. For daily maximum European AQI, Naive was the strongest baseline at the 1-day horizon, Holt Damped performed best at the 2-day horizon, and ARIMA performed best at the 3-day horizon. For daily mean PM2.5, ARIMA remained the strongest baseline at all three horizons. The AQI baseline mean MAE values increased from 3.12 at the 1-day horizon to 4.15 at 2 days and 4.49 at 3 days. For daily mean PM2.5, the corresponding baseline mean MAE values were 2.05, 3.00, and 2.78.

This baseline result is important for interpreting the rest of the benchmark. Because the baseline family already included persistence, drift, exponential smoothing, ARIMA, and lagged Random Forest models, the enhanced benchmark was evaluated against nontrivial local comparators rather than weak reference rules.

### B. Enhanced Benchmark Results

The enhanced benchmark produced the clearest gains for daily maximum European AQI. As shown in Table 3, HistGradientBoosting Global Features was the best enhanced model at all three AQI horizons. Figure 1 compares the best baseline and best enhanced models for each target and forecast horizon. Relative to the best baseline model, the enhanced benchmark reduced mean MAE from 3.12 to 2.82 at 1 day, from 4.15 to 3.84 at 2 days, and from 4.49 to 4.29 at 3 days. The corresponding MAPE improvements were also positive at all three horizons. Because both benchmark families were evaluated with the same 365-day lookback and 28-day origin spacing, Table 3 provides a direct family-level comparison under a common rolling-origin design. These AQI gains were consistent in the cross-city average summary, although their magnitude remained modest, especially at the 3-day horizon. Figure 1 shows that the average AQI advantage persisted across the full short-horizon forecast range rather than being limited to a single step ahead. Supplementary Table S5 reports the city-level AQI MAE deltas underlying this comparison: the enhanced benchmark yielded positive MAE improvement in 6 of 8 cities at the 1-day horizon and in 4 of 8 cities at both the 2-day and 3-day horizons. This supplementary robustness view supports the directional AQI result, but it does not replace formal uncertainty quantification.

The PM2.5 results were more mixed, which is consistent with the target-dependent modeling logic introduced in Section IV. At the 1-day horizon, the best enhanced model improved on the best baseline (mean MAE 1.92 versus 2.05). At the 2-day horizon, the gap favored the baseline (3.19 versus 3.00). At the 3-day horizon, the difference was negligible and slightly favored the baseline (2.79 versus 2.78). Taken together, these results show that pooled feature-based learning yielded consistent average gains for AQI forecasting, whereas PM2.5 forecasting remained mixed and horizon-dependent.

### C. Supplementary XGBoost Comparison

The supplementary XGBoost comparison provided additional context for the enhanced benchmark. Under the full 18-origin rolling evaluation and the same pooled feature design, XGBoost performed clearly better than HistGradientBoosting for PM2.5 at the 1-day horizon, reducing mean MAE from 1.9219 to 1.7767 and winning all 8 city-level comparisons. It also performed slightly better for AQI at the 2-day and 3-day horizons, although these gains were modest. However, XGBoost did not outperform the strongest existing enhanced model across all target-horizon combinations, so it should be interpreted as a competitive challenger rather than as a uniformly superior replacement.

The separate time-split tuning experiment led to a similarly selective conclusion. On the held-out evaluation origins, tuned XGBoost improved AQI forecasting at the 2-day horizon, reducing mean MAE from 2.5316 under the default XGBoost specification to 2.4306 and outperforming the held-out HistGradientBoosting incumbent (2.6756). For AQI at the 1-day horizon and PM2.5 at the 1-day and 3-day horizons, the selected configuration remained the default XGBoost setting. By contrast, tuning worsened AQI at the 3-day horizon and PM2.5 at the 2-day horizon relative to default XGBoost. Taken together, these supplementary results suggest that XGBoost is a credible challenger, especially for PM2.5 at the 1-day horizon and AQI at the 2-day horizon, but they do not support replacing the existing enhanced benchmark uniformly.

### D. Champion Selection Across Cities

Because the two benchmark families showed different strengths, the operational pipeline did not enforce a single winning model across all settings. Instead, it applied the champion-selection rule described in Section IV.E to each city, target, and forecast horizon. Figure 2 summarizes the resulting model-family wins across the eight cities.

Across all 48 city-target-horizon combinations, the enhanced family won 29 selections, whereas the baseline family won 19. For AQI, the enhanced family won 14 of 24 city-level selections: 6 of 8 at the 1-day horizon and 4 of 8 at both the 2-day and 3-day horizons. For PM2.5, the pattern also varied by horizon: the 1-day horizon favored the enhanced family (7 versus 1), the 2-day horizon favored the baseline family (6 versus 2), and the 3-day horizon again favored the enhanced family (6 versus 2). Figure 2 shows these counts directly, reporting for each target and forecast horizon how many of the eight cities selected the baseline or enhanced family as the operational champion.

The city-level pattern was also not driven by a single enhanced model. HistGradientBoosting Global Features accounted for many enhanced AQI wins, but local baseline models remained competitive in several AQI city-horizon combinations. For PM2.5, both HistGradientBoosting and Ridge appeared as enhanced champions, whereas ARIMA remained the main baseline winner. This pattern supports the central claim of the study: model deployment should be driven by benchmark evidence at the target-horizon level rather than fixed in advance.

### E. Illustrative Operational Case Study

Beyond benchmark accuracy, the workflow can be translated into illustrative operational alerts. For the fixed case study reported here, the forecast was issued on 28 April 2026 and covered the 1- to 3-day window from 29 April to 1 May 2026. The operational layer first selected, for each city, target, and horizon, the lower-MAE champion family identified in Section IV.E. It then generated AQI and PM2.5 forecasts for each city across the 1-, 2-, and 3-day horizons and converted every forecast row into a final alert level using predicted daily maximum AQI as the primary signal and predicted daily mean PM2.5 relative to the city-specific historical 90th and 95th percentiles as a supporting escalation rule. For each city, the workflow retained the single forecast row with the highest final alert score; when multiple horizons produced the same alert score, the row with the higher predicted AQI was retained. Table 4 reports these retained peak-risk rows, which is why each city appears only once in the ranking.

In this fixed snapshot, the PM2.5 support signal remained below the escalation thresholds for the retained city-level peak rows, so the final ranking was driven mainly by predicted AQI. Seven cities were assigned a Warning alert, whereas Korce was assigned Watch. Tirane ranked first because its retained peak-risk row occurred at the 3-day horizon and reached the highest predicted AQI (47.98), followed by Vlore (47.04), Fier (47.00), and Berat (47.00). Figure 3 presents the same retained city ranking in graphical form.

Figure 4 complements the ranking by showing the alert heatmap across cities and forecast dates for the same fixed case-study forecast, making the temporal distribution of short-horizon risk easier to inspect. Figure 5 adds a forecast-path view for the four highest-priority cities, linking the selected AQI champions to recent observed dynamics and projected short-term trajectories. Together, these outputs illustrate how benchmarked predictions can be translated into ranked, interpretable, and action-oriented information, rather than demonstrating retrospectively validated warning performance.

## VI. Discussion

The main benchmark finding of the study is that pooled feature-based learning was more effective for daily maximum European AQI than for daily mean PM2.5. In the cross-city average summary, the AQI target showed lower mean MAE under the enhanced benchmark at all three forecast horizons, although the city-level champion results show that this advantage was not universal and became more modest at the 3-day horizon. A plausible explanation, which should be treated as interpretive rather than causal, is that AQI as an integrated risk-oriented index may expose more shared temporal and meteorological structure across cities than the pollutant-specific PM2.5 target. Meteorological covariates were included in the enhanced AQI models and likely contributed to performance, but their isolated effect was not quantified in this study. This interpretation was not tested through ablation or feature-attribution analysis, but it is consistent with recent work showing that tree-based global forecasting models can perform strongly when temporal information is expressed through disciplined tabular features (Januschowski et al., 2022; Makridakis et al., 2022). The present evidence, however, should still be read as forecast-comparison evidence over open gridded air-quality fields rather than as direct validation against regulatory observations.

By contrast, the PM2.5 results reinforce the importance of target-specific and horizon-specific model comparison. The enhanced family performed best at the 1-day horizon, ARIMA regained the advantage at the 2-day horizon, and the 3-day comparison was nearly neutral and slightly favored the baseline. This pattern is consistent with broader methodological guidance that no single forecasting family should be assumed to dominate across all settings (Hewamalage et al., 2023; Strom & Gundersen, 2024), and with recent reviews showing that air-quality prediction performance remains strongly context-dependent despite rapid advances in machine learning and deep learning (Mendez et al., 2023; Agbehadji & Obagbuwa, 2024; Houdou et al., 2024). In practical terms, the PM2.5 findings argue against replacing local baselines simply because a more flexible pooled model is available.

The supplementary XGBoost experiments add a useful comparative nuance to this interpretation. They show that alternative tree-based global models can be competitive, especially for PM2.5 at the 1-day horizon and AQI at the 2-day horizon, but they do not support a universal replacement of the existing enhanced benchmark. In other words, the current results are better understood as evidence for a family of competitive pooled feature-based models than as evidence that one single boosted-tree implementation should dominate all targets and horizons.

The operational value of the framework therefore lies less in identifying a universal winning algorithm and more in connecting benchmarking to deployment decisions. The champion-selection layer formalizes that link by selecting the lower-error family for each city, target, and horizon, rather than fixing a single model across the full system. This design choice helps explain why the forecasting component remained useful even when the two target variables behaved differently. The alert outputs in this article should be read as an illustrative case study of workflow translation rather than as a retrospectively validated warning service. Even with that qualification, the study is positioned closer to reproducible forecasting systems and decision-support pipelines (Ke et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2024; "Moving towards reproducible machine learning," 2021) than to purely architecture-driven prediction studies (Gao et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025; Cengil, 2025; Li et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2025; Zaini et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2025). For a data-constrained open-data setting such as Albania, this emphasis on transparency, rerunnability, and operational interpretation remains a meaningful contribution, provided that the workflow is understood as operating over gridded proxy fields rather than validated station observations.

## VII. Limitations and Future Work

### A. Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the air-quality and meteorological inputs used in this study are open gridded products rather than regulatory ground-station measurements (Open-Meteo, 2026a, 2026b). The framework should therefore be interpreted as a reproducible forecasting workflow over modeled environmental fields, not as direct observational validation of urban air quality. Second, the analysis covers only eight cities and a relatively short study period, which limits the diversity of rare or extreme pollution episodes available for model training and evaluation. Third, the benchmark was designed to provide transparent and comparable evidence rather than exhaustive model optimization; accordingly, the reported results should be interpreted as benchmark evidence under a common rolling-origin design. Finally, the alert layer is presented as an illustrative operational case study rather than as a retrospectively validated warning service, and it does not yet include uncertainty-aware outputs or direct health-outcome modeling.

### B. Future Work

These limitations suggest several directions for future work. A first priority is external validation against regulatory monitoring stations where such observations are available, together with prospective testing in a live forecasting setting. A second priority is methodological extension through richer exogenous inputs and stronger spatial structure, including satellite products, low-cost sensor streams, traffic or emissions proxies, and hybrid regional models (Agbehadji & Obagbuwa, 2024; Li et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025). A further priority is to strengthen operational reliability through uncertainty-aware forecasting, online performance monitoring, and more explicit environmental-health response mapping (Rajesh et al., 2025; Ke et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2024; Strom & Gundersen, 2024).

## VIII. Conclusion

This study developed a reproducible workflow for short-term forecasting over open gridded environmental fields in eight Albanian cities and evaluated it through benchmark-driven prediction of daily mean PM2.5 and daily maximum European AQI. Within a common rolling-origin design, pooled feature-based models improved AQI forecasting in the cross-city average summary at all horizons, whereas PM2.5 forecasting remained mixed and horizon-dependent, with strong local baselines still competitive. These findings support a deployment strategy based on target- and horizon-specific champion selection rather than a single fixed model family. More broadly, the study shows that transparent benchmarking, open environmental data, and an illustrative alerting layer can be combined into a practical workflow for data-constrained urban settings, while external validation against regulatory observations remains an important next step.
